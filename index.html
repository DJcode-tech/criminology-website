Understanding Crime vs Deviance

Same world, different rules: laws vs norms.

What is Crime?

Crime is behavior that violates formal laws created and enforced by the state. Crimes are punishable through legal systems (police, courts, corrections). What counts as crime can change as laws change.

Key idea
Crime = written rule broken (law).

What is Deviance?

Deviance is behavior that violates social norms—unwritten expectations of what a group considers acceptable. Deviance varies across cultures and communities, and it is not always illegal.

Key idea
Deviance = social rule broken (norm).

Why the difference matters

Criminology asks more than “what happened?” It asks who is harmed, why certain acts are labeled criminal, and why enforcement differs across people and places. This matters especially for the dark figure of crime—harm that exists but never shows up in official statistics.

Types of Crime

A practical framework used in criminology.

Violent Crime

Crimes involving force or threat of force against a person.

Examples: assault, robbery, homicide

Property Crime

Crimes involving theft or damage of property, usually without direct violence.

Examples: burglary, theft, vandalism

White-Collar Crime

Non-violent financial crimes often committed in professional settings through deception.

Examples: fraud, embezzlement

Cybercrime

Crimes where computers/networks are the tool, target, or environment.

Examples: phishing, identity theft, ransomware

Organized Crime

Criminal activity coordinated by structured groups for profit or power.

Examples: trafficking networks, illegal gambling

Public Order Crime

Acts criminalized to maintain “order,” often based on moral/public norms.

Examples: disorderly conduct (varies by law)

Post: Different Types of Crime

Definitions • Victims & Offenders • Public Awareness • Examples

Violent Crime

  • Definition: Force or threat of force against a person.
  • Victims & offenders: Victims face physical + psychological harm; offenders range from acquaintances to strangers.
  • Public awareness: High—often heavily covered by media.
  • Examples: assault, armed robbery, homicide.

Property Crime

  • Definition: Taking or damaging property without direct violence against a person.
  • Victims & offenders: Individuals and businesses; offenders often act opportunistically.
  • Public awareness: Medium—common but less sensational.
  • Examples: burglary, theft, motor vehicle theft.

White-Collar Crime

  • Definition: Financial crime committed through deception in professional settings.
  • Victims & offenders: Customers, employees, shareholders, and the public; offenders may be high-status.
  • Public awareness: Medium–Low—often complex and less visible.
  • Examples: fraud, embezzlement, insider trading.

Cybercrime

  • Definition: Crimes using digital systems as tool/target.
  • Victims & offenders: Anyone online; offenders can be individuals or coordinated groups.
  • Public awareness: Increasing—especially scams and data breaches.
  • Examples: phishing, identity theft, ransomware.

Case Overview

A coordinated group sent messages pretending to be a bank, using urgency (“account locked”) to push victims to click a link. The link led to a realistic fake login page designed to capture credentials and one-time codes.

How the crime worked (step-by-step)

  1. Victim receives a realistic text/email with time pressure.
  2. Victim clicks a link to a fake website that mimics an official bank page.
  3. Victim enters login credentials and (sometimes) verification codes.
  4. Offenders access the account, transfer funds, or sell stolen data.
Victims
Everyday users—often busy, stressed, or unfamiliar with scam tactics.
Offenders
Groups using social engineering + technical setup (domains, fake pages, money mules).

Impact

  • Financial loss (direct theft)
  • Emotional stress and loss of trust
  • Time cost (bank calls, recovery, monitoring)

Why Crime Goes Unreported

Personal reasons + social reasons (the “dark figure of crime”).

Personal Reasons

  • Fear of retaliation: worrying the offender will come back.
  • Shame/embarrassment: especially for scams, sexual violence, domestic violence.
  • Minimizing harm: “It’s not serious enough.”
  • Distrust in outcomes: expecting reporting to be stressful or pointless.

Social Reasons

  • Stigma and victim-blaming: pressure to stay silent.
  • Barriers to access: language, time, transportation, legal knowledge.
  • Negative prior experiences: fear of not being believed or treated fairly.
  • Power dynamics: offender has influence (workplace, school, family, community).

2 Personal Reasons (Examples)

  • Fear of retaliation: A student avoids reporting bullying/assault because they fear escalation.
  • Shame/embarrassment: A scam victim doesn’t report because they feel “stupid,” even though the scam was designed to trick people.

2 Social Reasons (Examples)

  • Community stigma: A victim is pressured to “keep it private” to protect family reputation.
  • Access barriers: A worker doesn’t report wage theft due to language barriers and fear of losing their job.

Key point

Underreporting isn’t just a personal decision—it often reflects fear, stigma, and barriers that reduce safety and trust.

Consequences of Unreported Crime

What underreporting does to victims, communities, and policy.

Misleading crime statistics

Official data underestimates harm, causing resource misallocation and weak responses.

Repeat offending increases

No report often means no investigation—reducing risk for offenders and enabling repeats.

Victims miss support

Reporting can unlock services like protection orders, counseling referrals, and compensation.

Institutional trust falls

When people expect dismissal or harm from reporting, cooperation declines and safety worsens.

Policy becomes weaker

Hidden harms (DV, harassment, fraud) get ignored because the data looks “low.”

Community impact spreads

Unaddressed harm changes behavior: fear, avoidance, and normalization of abuse.

Post: The Real Costs of Unreported Crime

Consequences + examples to support each (required).

  • Misleading statistics: If school harassment is not reported, leadership may claim the campus is “safe,” delaying policy changes.
  • Repeat offenders: An abuser continues harming others because earlier incidents were never documented or reported.
  • Victims without support: A cyber scam victim doesn’t report and never receives guidance on freezing credit or recovering identity documents.
  • Community distrust: If people see cases dismissed, they stop cooperating with investigations, making everyone less safe.

Bottom line

Underreporting hides harm, lowers accountability, and blocks real solutions.

Why Students Should Take Criminology

Promoting the class with real value (not cheesy).

What you learn

  • Critical thinking: analyze causes, patterns, and bias using evidence.
  • Media literacy: separate facts from fear-driven headlines.
  • Systems thinking: understand law, inequality, justice, and prevention.
  • Real-life relevance: online safety, relationships, and community responsibility.

Where it can lead

  • Law, psychology, forensics
  • Cybersecurity and fraud prevention
  • Public policy and community safety
  • Journalism, research, and social work
Final message
Criminology helps you understand harm—and how societies reduce it.
