Crime vs Deviance

Crime

Crime is behavior that breaks the law. Laws are written rules created by governments. If someone commits a crime, the legal system can punish them through fines, jail, or other penalties.

Deviance

Deviance is behavior that goes against social expectations. Something can be deviant without being illegal. For example, acting extremely rude in public might be socially unacceptable but not criminal.

Types of Crime

Violent Crime

Crimes involving force or the threat of force such as assault or robbery.

Property Crime

Crimes where someone takes or damages property without using violence.

Cybercrime

Crimes that happen online such as scams, hacking, or identity theft.

Case Study: Online Banking Scam

A large phishing scam targeted bank customers by sending messages that looked like official bank alerts. Victims were told their accounts had security issues and were asked to log in immediately.

The provided link led to a fake website designed to steal login information. Once criminals gained access, they transferred money out of victims’ accounts.

Cases like this show how crime is evolving. Technology allows offenders to reach thousands of people quickly, while victims often feel embarrassed and never report what happened.

Why Take Criminology?

Criminology helps students understand real social problems. Instead of relying on stereotypes or headlines, the subject looks at evidence, patterns, and causes of crime.

Students interested in law, psychology, politics, journalism, or cybersecurity often find criminology useful because it connects theory with real world issues.

Why Crimes Go Unreported

Crime statistics often give the impression that they represent everything that happens in society. However, criminologists know that many crimes never enter official records. This hidden portion is called the “dark figure of crime.” Understanding why victims do not report crime is important because it changes how governments, researchers, and communities understand safety and justice.

When crimes are not reported, offenders may continue harming others and policymakers may underestimate serious problems. The reasons behind this silence usually fall into two major categories: personal reasons and social reasons.

Personal Reasons

One major reason people do not report crimes is fear. Victims may worry that the offender will retaliate, especially if the person knows where they live, study, or work. This is particularly common in domestic violence, bullying, or gang-related incidents.

Another personal factor is shame or embarrassment. Victims of scams or harassment sometimes blame themselves for what happened. Because of this, they choose not to talk about the incident with authorities or even with friends and family.

In addition, some people believe reporting crime will not lead to any real consequences. If victims think the police cannot solve the case or that the process will take too much time, they may decide it is not worth the effort.

Social Reasons

Beyond personal feelings, broader social pressures also influence reporting decisions. In some communities, victims may face stigma or judgment for speaking out. Instead of receiving support, they may worry about damaging their reputation or causing conflict within families or communities.

Another factor is trust. If people feel that law enforcement institutions are ineffective, biased, or difficult to work with, they may avoid reporting crimes altogether. Historical experiences with corruption or discrimination can deepen this distrust.

Finally, structural barriers such as language differences, immigration concerns, or lack of knowledge about legal rights can prevent victims from reporting crimes. These barriers are especially significant for migrant workers and vulnerable populations.

Examples of Unreported Crime

Personal Reason Examples

Online scam victims: Many people who lose money through phishing scams never report the crime. They often feel embarrassed or assume that nothing can be done to recover the money. Because of this, official cybercrime statistics may be much lower than the real number of cases.

School bullying: Students frequently avoid reporting bullying because they fear retaliation or believe teachers will not take the situation seriously. This silence allows bullying behavior to continue and sometimes escalate.

Social Reason Examples

Domestic violence: In some communities, victims are pressured by family members to keep abuse private in order to protect family reputation. This social pressure prevents victims from seeking help and allows abuse to continue.

Migrant worker exploitation: Workers who face wage theft or unsafe conditions may avoid reporting their employers because they fear losing their job or facing immigration consequences.

Consequences of Unreported Crime

When crimes are not reported, the consequences extend far beyond the individual victim. Entire communities and institutions are affected.

Inaccurate Crime Data

Governments rely on crime statistics to decide where to allocate funding and police resources. If large numbers of crimes go unreported, policymakers may underestimate serious issues. For example, if cybercrime victims rarely report incidents, governments may fail to invest enough in digital security programs.

Repeat Offending

When offenders are not reported, they are less likely to face consequences. This allows them to continue committing crimes against others. Fraud networks and online scam groups often rely on the fact that many victims never report the crime.

Victims Lose Access to Support

Many support services such as legal protection, counseling, and financial compensation depend on official reports. Victims who remain silent may not receive the help they need to recover.

Loss of Public Trust

When people believe reporting crime will not make a difference, trust in institutions declines. This can weaken cooperation between communities and law enforcement, making crime prevention more difficult in the long term.

Crime in Indonesia

Indonesia is one of the largest countries in the world with a diverse population and rapidly growing cities. Crime patterns in Indonesia reflect both urban challenges and economic changes.

Common Crimes

The most frequently reported crimes include theft, robbery, fraud, and drug-related offenses. Property crimes tend to make up a large portion of police reports, particularly in densely populated cities. Cybercrime has also increased as internet use has expanded.

Famous Crime Case

One widely discussed case in Indonesia was the cyanide coffee murder involving Jessica Kumala Wongso in 2016. The case attracted massive national attention and media coverage. A woman died after drinking coffee that had allegedly been poisoned with cyanide. Investigators argued that the suspect carefully planned the crime, while the trial sparked debates about forensic evidence and the legal process.

This case relates to violent crime and highlights how personal conflicts, psychological factors, and media attention can shape public perception of crime.

Government Initiatives to Reduce Crime

Governments implement various strategies to reduce crime, including stronger law enforcement, community programs, and technological monitoring.

Example Initiative

Indonesia has expanded cybercrime units within the national police to respond to increasing online fraud and digital offenses. Authorities have also introduced public awareness campaigns to educate citizens about online scams and digital safety.

Is It Working?

While these programs have helped improve detection and awareness, cybercrime continues to grow as technology evolves. Many victims still hesitate to report scams, which makes it difficult for authorities to track offenders.

Suggestions for Improvement

  • Increase public education about reporting cybercrime
  • Strengthen international cooperation to track global scammers
  • Improve victim support systems
  • Invest in advanced digital investigation tools

Why Students Should Take Criminology

Criminology offers students a deeper understanding of how societies deal with crime. Instead of relying on assumptions or sensational news stories, the class examines real evidence, research, and case studies.

Throughout the course, students analyze different types of crime, discuss real cases, and explore how justice systems operate. Activities may include case analysis, debates, research projects, and discussions about crime prevention strategies.

One of the most interesting aspects of the class is learning how social, economic, and psychological factors influence criminal behavior. Students begin to see crime not just as individual wrongdoing, but as part of larger social patterns.

For students interested in careers such as law, psychology, journalism, cybersecurity, or public policy, criminology provides valuable insights into real-world issues.
