Understanding Crime Beyond Headlines

Crime statistics tell part of the story. Criminology looks deeper — what counts as crime, why people offend, why many incidents never get reported, and how society responds.

Foundations

Crime vs Deviance

People often use the words crime and deviance like they mean the same thing, but they don’t. Criminology separates them because laws and social norms do different jobs in society — and they can change over time.

Crime

Crime is behavior that breaks the law. Laws are written rules created by governments. If someone commits a crime, the legal system can punish them through fines, prison, or other penalties. What counts as crime can change as laws change (for example, digital privacy laws and cybercrime laws).

Crime matters because it triggers formal systems: police reports, investigations, courts, and sentencing.

Deviance

Deviance is behavior that violates social expectations — even if it’s not illegal. Something can be deviant in one culture and normal in another.

Example: acting extremely rude in public may be socially unacceptable (deviant) but not criminal. At the same time, some illegal behaviors can become “normalized” in certain groups.

Why the difference matters

Criminology doesn’t only ask “what happened?” It asks who decides what counts as crime, how society labels behavior, and how enforcement can differ depending on power, media attention, and social status. This becomes even more important when crimes are not reported — because official statistics can become misleading.

Overview

Types of Crime

Crime can be grouped by who is harmed, what the motive is, and how it is carried out. These categories help criminologists compare patterns and design better prevention strategies.

Violent Crime

Crimes involving force or threat of force. These cases usually create high fear and attract major media attention.
Examples: assault, robbery, homicide.

Property Crime

Crimes where someone takes, damages, or enters property without permission. These are common in urban areas.
Examples: theft, burglary, vehicle theft, vandalism.

Cybercrime

Crimes that happen online or use digital systems as the tool or target. These can scale quickly and cross borders.
Examples: phishing, hacking, identity theft, ransomware.

Why “type” matters

Different crimes have different victim profiles, different reporting rates, and different evidence. For example, cybercrime is often underreported because victims feel embarrassed, don’t know where to report, or assume nothing will happen. In contrast, violent crime is more likely to be reported because the harm is visible and urgent.

Real Case Study

Case Study: The WannaCry Ransomware Attack (May 2017)

The WannaCry ransomware attack was one of the most disruptive cybercrime events in modern history. In May 2017, WannaCry spread rapidly across networks by exploiting a Windows vulnerability in SMB (Server Message Block). Once it infected a computer, it encrypted files and demanded payment in bitcoin to restore access.

Type of Crime

Cybercrime — ransomware / digital extortion. The crime involves locking victims out of their data (encryption) and pressuring them to pay for a decryption key. It’s a form of extortion because the “threat” is losing access to files permanently.

WannaCry was also a worm (self-spreading malware), meaning it could move quickly through vulnerable systems without needing each victim to click a link.

Victims & Offenders

Victims: organizations and individuals using unpatched Windows systems. High-impact victims included parts of the UK’s NHS, where disruption led to cancelled appointments and operational chaos.

Offenders: the exact individuals were not publicly confirmed in the same way as traditional crimes, but investigations by governments and security agencies widely discussed attribution. Regardless of who did it, the criminology point is that cybercrime can be executed remotely and at scale.

How WannaCry Spread (Mechanism)

WannaCry spread by exploiting a vulnerability in Windows networking (SMB). Microsoft released a security update (MS17-010) in March 2017, but many organizations had not installed it. That gap in patching created a massive “opportunity structure” for offenders: once the malware hit one vulnerable machine, it could spread across connected systems.

Why this matters for criminology: many crimes are not only about motivation — they’re also about opportunity. Here, outdated systems + weak patching practices created the opportunity.

Ransom Demand & Pressure Tactics

WannaCry demanded roughly $300 in bitcoin per machine and increased pressure by threatening higher costs over time. This is a classic technique in extortion: urgency reduces careful thinking and pushes victims to act quickly.

Public Awareness & Underreporting

WannaCry received huge attention because it hit major institutions like hospitals, which makes the harm visible to the public. But many cybercrime victims still do not report incidents, especially individuals and small businesses. Victims may feel embarrassed, think reporting is pointless, or not know which agency to contact — contributing to the dark figure of crime in cybercrime.

Key Concept

Why Crimes Go Unreported

Crime statistics rely on one major step: someone must report the incident. Criminologists call unrecorded incidents the dark figure of crime — harm that happened but never became an official case. Underreporting matters because it changes policy decisions, resource allocation, and how the public understands safety.

Reporting decisions are shaped by personal reasons (individual fear, emotions, or cost of reporting) and social reasons (stigma, trust in authorities, and barriers).

Personal Reasons

Fear of retaliation: victims worry the offender will return or escalate.

Shame or embarrassment: scam victims may feel “stupid” even though scams are designed to trick people.

Low confidence in results: victims believe police can’t solve it, especially for minor theft or online crimes.

Emotional exhaustion: reporting can feel like reliving the experience, especially in harassment or abuse cases.

Social Reasons

Stigma: victims fear judgment, gossip, or blame.

Distrust of authorities: if people expect dismissal or unfair treatment, they avoid reporting.

Access barriers: language, time, transportation, legal knowledge, or disability access issues.

Power imbalance: when the offender has influence (workplace/family/community), victims feel trapped.

Required Examples

Examples of Unreported Crime

Below are examples showing two personal reasons and two social reasons why crimes go unreported. These are linked directly to the reporting barriers explained above.

Personal (2 Examples)

Example 1 — Online scam victims: a person loses money after entering details on a fake login page. They don’t report because they feel embarrassed and assume nothing will be recovered. Some fear being blamed by family for “falling for it.”

Example 2 — School bullying/harassment: a student experiences repeated harassment but stays silent because they fear retaliation, social isolation, or not being taken seriously. Silence allows the behavior to continue and escalate.

Social (2 Examples)

Example 1 — Domestic violence & reputation pressure: victims are pressured to keep abuse private to protect the family’s reputation. The social pressure becomes a barrier to safety, which helps abuse continue.

Example 2 — Migrant worker exploitation: a worker experiences wage theft or unsafe conditions but avoids reporting due to fear of job loss, legal consequences, or not being believed because of language barriers.

Impact

Consequences of Unreported Crime

When crimes are not reported, the harm doesn’t disappear — it becomes harder to measure and harder to stop. Underreporting affects victims directly, but it also reshapes how society responds to crime.

1) Inaccurate crime data

Governments rely on reported crime to decide funding and prevention strategy. If a crime category is heavily underreported (like online scams), it can look “small” on paper, meaning fewer resources are assigned to it.

2) Repeat offending becomes easier

Reporting increases the chance of investigation. Without reports, offenders face lower risk and continue targeting new victims. Many scam networks rely on silence to keep operating.

3) Victims lose access to support

Support services (legal help, counseling, documentation for compensation) are often easier to access with an official report. Scam victims may need documentation for bank disputes, and abuse victims may need documentation for protection.

4) Public trust declines

If people believe reporting is useless or harmful, cooperation drops. Over time, this weakens relationships between communities and institutions and makes prevention harder.

Policy

Government Initiative in Indonesia

Governments reduce crime through enforcement, prevention, and public education. A major growing challenge is cybercrime, especially scams that target ordinary people.

What the initiative is about

A common approach is strengthening cybercrime investigation capacity: dedicated units, digital forensics, and awareness campaigns that teach citizens how to spot fake links and urgent “security alert” messages.

Is it working? If not, what went wrong?

Cybercrime keeps growing because offenders adapt quickly. Problems happen when reporting systems are confusing, victims feel embarrassed, or awareness campaigns don’t reach groups most targeted (older people, new internet users).

Another challenge: many scams are cross-border, which makes investigation harder without international coordination.

Suggestions to improve the initiative

  • Simplify reporting: one clear national portal/hotline for cybercrime with step-by-step guidance.
  • Normalize reporting: campaigns that reduce shame and show reporting protects others.
  • Better victim support: quick instructions for banks, evidence capture, and recovery steps.
  • Targeted education: school lessons + community sessions for high-risk groups.
  • Stronger cooperation: coordinate with banks/telecoms/international agencies to trace networks faster.

Case Timeline

Timeline: WannaCry Ransomware (2017)

A timeline helps show how a cybercrime event escalates quickly: the vulnerability exists, malware spreads, systems fail, and the response reveals weak points.

March 2017 — Patch released

Microsoft publishes a security update (MS17-010) to fix a serious Windows SMB vulnerability. Many organizations delay patching due to legacy systems, operational risk, or poor IT management.

12 May 2017 — Outbreak begins

WannaCry spreads rapidly across networks. Computers become locked as files are encrypted, and a ransom note demands bitcoin to restore access.

Same day — Major disruption (including NHS)

Hospitals and clinics in the UK experience major service disruption. Appointments are cancelled and staff lose access to systems. This is why WannaCry became a public-facing cybercrime event, not just a “tech problem.”

Containment — Kill switch discovered

A security researcher discovers a “kill switch” behavior in the malware that slows the spread once triggered. Even with containment, recovery takes time: systems must be rebuilt and policies re-evaluated.

Aftermath — Lessons and prevention

The case highlights how prevention is often about basic security practices: patching, backups, and network segmentation. It also shows how cybercrime can create a large “dark figure” when many victims (especially individuals) never report incidents.

Class Promotion

Why Students Should Take Criminology

Criminology is interesting because it explains real-world problems with evidence — not just opinions. Instead of relying on stereotypes or headlines, it looks at patterns, causes, victim experiences, and the effectiveness of solutions.

What I’ve learned so far

Crime is shaped by opportunity, social conditions, relationships, economics, and enforcement — not just “bad people.” I also learned that crime statistics can be misleading when crimes go unreported, which is why criminologists study the dark figure of crime.

What makes the class interesting

The class feels real because it uses cases, debates, and real examples. You can connect the content to everyday situations: scams, online safety, school conflict, and justice system fairness.

Examples of class activities + benefits

  • Case analysis: break down a case step-by-step and explain motive, opportunity, and consequences.
  • Debates: compare solutions (punishment vs prevention, rehabilitation, fairness).
  • Research posts: write evidence-based explanations of crime types and underreporting.
  • Benefit: stronger critical thinking, better media literacy, and a deeper understanding of society.
